<section id="pennsylvania">
<h3>Pennsylvania: center-left</h3>
<p>Pennsylvania ranks 23rd of 51, with a score of 73.5, tied with Ohio. That is center-left on this scale: well short of Massachusetts or Connecticut, and well short of a pure “you signed it” state. The split is the point. The Unfair Trade Practices and Consumer Protection Law is a real private right, but Pennsylvania contract doctrine still treats the integrated writing as the deal.</p>
<p>On the consumer-protective side, the UTPCPL, 73 P.S. § 201-1 et seq., covers trade or commerce, including credit, and its catch-all, § 201-2(4)(xxi), reaches “deceptive conduct.” A private plaintiff who proves an ascertainable loss may recover actual damages, up to treble damages, and attorney fees under § 201-9.2. In <cite>Gregg v. Ameriprise Financial</cite>, 247 A.3d 1043 (Pa. 2021), the Pennsylvania Supreme Court held that this catch-all imposes strict liability for deceptive conduct: the vendor need not have intended to deceive or have been negligent. Conduct with the capacity to deceive is enough, if the consumer justifiably relied and suffered financial harm. That holding is as advertised, with those two limits still attached.</p>
<p>On the sanctity-of-contract side, justifiable reliance is a required element of a private UTPCPL claim (<cite>Toy v. Metropolitan Life Ins. Co.</cite>, 928 A.2d 186 (Pa. 2007); <cite>Yocca</cite>). In <cite>Yocca v. Pittsburgh Steelers Sports, Inc.</cite>, 854 A.2d 425 (Pa. 2004), the Court applied a strict parol-evidence rule to an integrated stadium-license agreement. The writing superseded earlier marketing, and fraud in the inducement generally cannot be used to vary an integrated writing. The same integration clause defeated justifiable reliance on those pre-contract statements for the UTPCPL claim. Pennsylvania also uses the gist-of-the-action doctrine (see <cite>Bruno v. Erie Insurance</cite>) to keep many disputes in contract rather than tort. NCLC rated Pennsylvania “Undecided” on whether the UTPCPL contains a broad, privately enforceable unfairness or unconscionability prohibition going beyond deception. <cite>Gregg</cite> is a deception case, not a Chapter 93A-style power to rewrite harsh but disclosed terms.</p>
<p>For mortgage work, Pennsylvania is a judicial-foreclosure state, and Chapter 5 of the Mortgage Bankers and Brokers and Consumer Equity Protection Act adds high-cost-loan limits. Net: Pennsylvania will police deception in a consumer transaction, including after the signing, more readily than many states. It is less willing than Massachusetts to undo a contract simply because the terms are unfair.</p>

<section id="left-pole">
<h3>The consumer-protective pole</h3>
<p>The left end of this scale is not a political ranking. It marks jurisdictions where a statute or doctrine is most likely to reach beyond the four corners of a signed consumer contract, with remedies that make a challenge realistic.</p>
<p>Massachusetts, at 91.2 and second on the list, is the classic example of that pole. Chapter 93A prohibits unfair or deceptive acts in trade or commerce. The Attorney General’s regulation, 940 CMR 3.16, says a practice violates Chapter 93A if it is “oppressive or otherwise unconscionable in any respect,” if it conceals a fact that might have changed the buyer’s mind, or if it violates certain other consumer-protection laws. That is a statutory hook to treat oppressive terms as unfair, not merely to police lies. Consumers generally must send a 30-day demand letter. Willful or knowing violations, or a bad-faith refusal to settle, can bring double or treble damages plus fees. Massachusetts also has the Predatory Home Loan Practices Act, ch. 183C. One offset: customary foreclosure is nonjudicial (power of sale). 93A is the left pole; the foreclosure path is not.</p>
<p>Connecticut ranks first at 93.0. The Connecticut Unfair Trade Practices Act, Conn. Gen. Stat. § 42-110a et seq., is a broad little-FTC act with a private right, fee-shifting, and discretionary punitive damages. NCLC rated its prohibitions and private access as strong, with only a mixed mark on insurance and on the size of public civil penalties. Customary foreclosure is judicial, and Connecticut has an abusive-home-loan statute at §§ 36a-746 et seq.</p>
<p>New Jersey is third at 91.0. The Consumer Fraud Act, N.J. Stat. § 56:8-1 et seq., is known for mandatory treble damages and attorney fees. The Home Ownership Security Act, N.J.S.A. 46:10B-22 et seq., adds high-cost mortgage rules, and foreclosure is judicial. Close behind are New Mexico (Unfair Practices Act plus the Home Loan Protection Act) and Hawaii, which was the only state NCLC rated “Strong” on every feature it scored, including automatic treble damages or $1,000 and coverage of lenders. North Dakota, Illinois, Vermont, Missouri, and California also land in this far-left band. A high statutory-feature score, as in North Dakota, is not the same thing as a Chapter 93A reputation; the rubric measures the tools on the books, not how often courts actually rewrite deals.</p>
</section>

<section id="right-pole">
<h3>The sanctity-of-contract pole</h3>
<p>The right end is where private consumers have the hardest time using a UDAP statute to get out from under, or to reprice, a signed deal: narrow or unenforceable catch-alls, industry exemptions (especially for lenders), proof obstacles, or remedies that do not make a winner whole.</p>
<p>Mississippi is last at 38.0. NCLC found that consumers cannot enforce the statute’s broad prohibitions, and that attorney fees and multiple damages are lacking. It called Mississippi, with Iowa’s private-action gaps, among the weakest overall private-remedy regimes. Iowa itself scores much higher here than that 2018 narrative might suggest, because chapter 714H now supplies treble damages and fees for the businesses it does cover; the private right still does not reach most banks, credit unions, insurers, or real-estate licensees, which is why Iowa is not on the left pole.</p>
<p>Virginia, at 47.5, has no general unfairness prohibition and exempts most lenders, insurers, and utilities from the Consumer Protection Act. Willful violations can be trebled, but the coverage holes dominate for mortgage and credit work. South Dakota, at 50.5, lacks an unfairness catch-all, often requires knowledge or intent for deception, and does not award attorney fees in individual consumer suits. Tennessee and Florida sit just inside this far-right band: Tennessee consumers cannot enforce the broad prohibitions; Florida’s FDUTPA is broad on paper but carves out lenders and can require an unsuccessful plaintiff to pay the business’s fees. Colorado and Minnesota, just above the cut, are laundry-list or narrow-deception jurisdictions without a general unfairness catch-all.</p>
</section>

<section id="caveats">
<h3>Caveats</h3>
<p>Scores are a synthesis, not a court. They compress statutes, remedies, proof rules, a few contract doctrines, foreclosure path, and selected high-cost-loan laws into one number. That is useful for orientation and dangerous if treated as a prediction about a particular loan or clause.</p>
<p>The UDAP backbone is NCLC’s 2018 feature ratings. Law has moved since then. Michigan 2026 and Rhode Island 2021 are called out because they are well documented; other amendments may not be reflected. Parol-evidence and economic-loss adjustments were made only for documented outliers (Pennsylvania, Wisconsin, plus the Massachusetts and California unfair-terms boosts). A state can be stricter or more liberal on those doctrines than the baseline assumes. The high-cost-loan list is conservative and not exhaustive. Some states allow both judicial and nonjudicial foreclosure; the table uses the customary path, and Maryland, Hawaii, and Oklahoma are scored as mixed.</p>
<p>Federal law sits on top of all of this. HOEPA, ability-to-repay and qualified-mortgage rules, and the TILA ban on mandatory arbitration clauses in residential mortgage loans, 15 U.S.C. § 1639c(e), apply nationwide. State differences still matter for UDAP claims, unfair terms, foreclosure procedure, and state high-cost statutes, but no state ranking replaces that federal floor. This page is for general briefing, not legal advice.</p>
</section>
